What Are the Strangest Animals in the Galápagos?

Published 2026-07-16 · By the Galapagos Islands.Travel editorial team, reviewed by Juan Magallanes (Galápagos Travel Advisor)

The Galápagos runs on strangeness for one reason: isolation bred animals found nowhere else. About 80% of its land birds and roughly 97% of its reptiles and land mammals are endemic. That gives you the only sea-feeding lizard, a cormorant that lost flight, and the only penguin living north of the equator.

Quick facts:

  • about 80% of land birds are endemic
  • about 97% of reptiles & land mammals are endemic
  • More than 1,300 endemic species overall
  • Several species live on a single island — nowhere else on Earth

On this page: The 10 species · Strange traits at a glance · Key takeaways · FAQ · Related guides

Why these animals are so strange

The short version: isolation. The Galápagos sit about 600 miles off the South American coast, and the islands run older in the east to youngest in the west. That age gradient, plus deep ocean on every side, produced animals that evolved in place — many on a single island and nowhere else.

So one archipelago holds a lizard that feeds in the sea, a cormorant that can't fly, and a penguin that lives on the equator. What follows is ten of the strangest, with the real biology behind each. No ranking — just ten genuinely odd animals.

The 10 strangest Galápagos species

1. Marine iguana — the only sea-going lizard

The marine iguana is the only lizard on Earth that feeds in the ocean, grazing algae off submerged rocks. It sneezes excess salt out through a nasal gland, leaving white crusts on its head. You'll find it on Isabela, Fernandina, Española, Floreana and Santa Cruz, with the largest, densest populations on Fernandina. In breeding season males turn red and green — the so-called "Christmas iguanas" of Española and Floreana.

2. Flightless cormorant — a seabird that gave up flight

With no land predators and rich cold seas below, this cormorant traded flight for diving. Its wings are stunted, useless in the air. It lives on the northwest coast of Isabela and eastern Fernandina — and nowhere else in the world. The whole species survives on roughly 1,600–2,400 birds.

3. Galápagos penguin — the only penguin north of the equator

This is the only penguin that lives at the equator, and part of its range crosses into the northern hemisphere. Cold currents make that possible. Most of it — about 95% — sticks to Fernandina and western Isabela, with smaller numbers on Bartolomé, Floreana and Santiago. The population is small and El Niño events crash their numbers.

4. Vampire finch — the finch that drinks blood

On remote Wolf Island, a ground finch pecks at the base of nesting seabirds' feathers and drinks their blood. The behaviour probably started with eating parasites off the birds. Wolf is one of the far-northern islands, reachable only by liveaboard dive trips, so few travellers ever see it.

5. Pink land iguana — found on one volcano, nowhere else

The pink land iguana lives only on Wolf Volcano at the northern tip of Isabela — a single population of roughly 350 animals, and Critically Endangered. It wasn't formally described until recent decades. Its range is permit-restricted, so it's effectively off-limits to visitors.

6. Waved albatross — a courtship dance on one island on Earth

Almost the entire world population of waved albatross nests on Española. Pairs perform an elaborate bill-clacking, sky-pointing courtship dance. They're on land roughly April to December and gone January to March, laying a single egg per year and sometimes skipping years.

7. Blue-footed booby — the feet, and what they signal

The blue-footed booby's vivid feet aren't decoration. Brighter feet signal a healthier, better-fed bird, and males show them off in a high-stepping courtship strut. Look for them on North Seymour, Española, Isabela and Floreana, with courtship and nesting around June to August.

8. Giant tortoise — temperature-set sex, century-plus lives

Giant tortoises can live well over a century, and nest temperature sets the sex of a hatchling — warmer nests produce females. About 12 species are alive today of the roughly 15 described. Hunted from a historical 200,000–300,000 down to about 20,000–25,000, some are recovering: Española's tortoises came back from just 14–15 animals. Lonesome George, the last Pinta tortoise, died in June 2012.

9. Galápagos fur seal — a "seal" that's actually a sea lion

Despite the name, the Galápagos fur seal is a sea lion, not a true seal. It favours cool, shaded, rocky shores and is best seen in the western islands, at James Bay on Santiago and Darwin Bay on Genovesa. It's the smaller and shier of the archipelago's two eared seals.

10. Red-footed booby — the booby that nests in trees

The red-footed booby is the smallest of the three Galápagos boobies and the only one that nests in trees rather than on the ground — its red feet grip the branches. The highest concentration is on Genovesa, with birds also on San Cristóbal, viewable roughly year-round. At Punta Pitt on San Cristóbal, all three booby species nest together — the only place they do.

Key takeaways

  • Isolation made the strangeness. About 80% of land birds and 97% of reptiles and land mammals are found only in the Galápagos.
  • Some species live on a single island. The pink land iguana (Wolf Volcano) and the vampire finch (Wolf) exist nowhere else.
  • The waved albatross's whole world population nests on Española.
  • Names can mislead: the fur seal is a sea lion.
  • Several are rare or endangered — the flightless cormorant (about 1,600–2,400) and pink iguana (about 350) among them.

Frequently asked questions

What is the strangest animal in the Galápagos?

There's no single answer, but the marine iguana is a strong contender: it's the only lizard on Earth that feeds in the sea, grazing algae off underwater rocks and sneezing out the salt it swallows. That diving, salt-sneezing lifestyle exists nowhere else.

Why are Galápagos animals so unusual and found nowhere else?

The islands sit about 600 miles offshore, isolated by deep ocean and ranging in age from older east to youngest west. Species arrived rarely, then evolved in place — often on a single island. The result: about 80% of land birds and 97% of reptiles are endemic.

Are there really penguins in the Galápagos?

Yes. The Galápagos penguin is the only penguin that lives at the equator, with part of its range in the northern hemisphere. About 95% live around Fernandina and western Isabela, with smaller numbers on Bartolomé, Floreana and Santiago. Cold currents make their tropical home possible.

Is there a finch that drinks blood?

Yes — the vampire finch on remote Wolf Island pecks at the base of nesting seabirds' feathers and drinks their blood, likely an evolution from eating parasites off the birds. Wolf is a far-northern island reachable only by liveaboard dive trips, so few travellers ever encounter it.

What is the pink iguana and where does it live?

The pink land iguana is a Critically Endangered species of only about 350 animals, living solely on Wolf Volcano at the northern tip of Isabela. Its range is permit-restricted and effectively closed to visitors — part of why it was only described in recent decades.

Why do marine iguanas sneeze or spit salt?

Because they feed in the sea, marine iguanas swallow a lot of salt. A special nasal gland concentrates it, and they "sneeze" it out in a fine spray — which often lands back on their heads, leaving the white crusts you'll see on basking animals.

Is a Galápagos fur seal a seal or a sea lion?

It's a sea lion, despite the name. The Galápagos fur seal is the smaller and shier of the archipelago's two eared seals, and it prefers cool, shaded, rocky shores. Look for it in the western islands, at James Bay on Santiago and Darwin Bay on Genovesa.

Which Galápagos animals are found only there?

Many. Overall the archipelago has more than 1,300 endemic species. Standouts include the marine iguana, flightless cormorant, Galápagos penguin, pink land iguana and vampire finch — the last two on a single volcano and island respectively. About 97% of reptiles and land mammals are endemic.

Are Galápagos animals dangerous or afraid of people?

They're famously unafraid, because they evolved with few predators — but that's not an invitation to approach. Park rules require staying at least 2 metres from wildlife and touring protected sites with a certified naturalist guide. Keep your distance and let them ignore you.

Can you see these species on any trip?

It depends on where and when you go. Many are tied to specific islands or seasons — waved albatross are on Española only about April to December, and Wolf's species need a dive liveaboard. Wildlife calendars are approximate, and no sighting is ever guaranteed.
