What Is the Galápagos Land Iguana?

Published by Galapagos Islands.Travel · Reviewed by Juan Magallanes, Galápagos Travel Advisor · Last updated 25 June 2026

The Galápagos land iguana is a large, yellowish, endemic lizard that lives on land and feeds mainly on Opuntia cactus — unlike the marine iguana, which feeds in the sea. Several islands hold it, some naturally and some through reintroduction. A separate pink land iguana survives only on Isabela's Wolf Volcano.

What is the Galápagos land iguana — and how is it different from the marine iguana?

The Galápagos land iguana is a large, yellowish lizard found only in the archipelago. It lives on land and eats plants. It is one of four iguana species here, a group that also includes the pink land iguana and the marine iguana.

The clearest difference is where each one feeds. The marine iguana is the only lizard in the world that feeds in the sea, grazing on algae underwater. The land iguana stays ashore and depends on cactus and other plants. Both are endemic reptiles, but they live in entirely different niches.

South Plaza is where the two overlap. It is the only place land and marine iguanas are known to interbreed, and its land iguanas are the smallest in the islands.

Which islands have Galápagos land iguanas?

Land iguanas live on Isabela, Fernandina, Santa Cruz, Baltra, South Plaza and North Seymour. But that single list hides three very different histories: some populations are native, some were returned after local extinction, and one was moved by people from another island.

Native populations

Fernandina is the youngest and most pristine island. It never had introduced mammals, and it holds the largest and densest marine and land iguanas; its single visitor site is Punta Espinoza. Isabela is also native land-iguana range and the sole home of the separate pink land iguana. Santa Cruz, South Plaza and Santa Fe carry native land iguanas too, and Santa Fe's is a distinct endemic form.

Reintroduced and introduced populations

Baltra's land iguanas went extinct around 1954 and were later reintroduced. Santiago's were reintroduced in 2019. North Seymour is different again: its land iguanas are not native — they were brought over from Baltra in the 1930s. North Seymour is otherwise best known for the largest magnificent-frigatebird colony and prime seabird breeding.

What is the pink land iguana, and can visitors see it?

The pink land iguana is a separate species that survives only on Wolf Volcano, Isabela — around 350 individuals, and Critically Endangered. Isabela is its only home.

In practice, travellers cannot see it. Its Wolf Volcano range is permit-restricted and closed to general visitors, and the National Park sites on that side of Isabela — including Volcán Alcedo — carry the lowest visitor quotas in the archipelago. Anyone marketing a “pink iguana” sighting on a standard cruise is overselling; it is not an encounter you can plan for.

What do land iguanas eat? The Opuntia connection

Galápagos land iguanas are herbivores, and their diet leans heavily on the Opuntia (prickly-pear) cactus — flowers, pads and fruit alike. Santa Fe shows the relationship well: the island pairs a distinct land iguana with giant Opuntia. On arid islands where standing water is scarce, the moisture and food packed into cactus make it central to the iguana's survival.

How do land iguanas breed?

Land iguanas mate from January, and the young hatch around April. That is the species' breeding biology, not a reason to pick one travel window over another — land iguanas are resident where they live, so you may encounter adults at any time.

The conservation story: extinction, breeding and return

Land iguanas show both how fragile island populations are and how they can recover. The Baltra extinction around 1954 came from the same pressures that have hit ground-dwelling reptiles across the archipelago; the population was later re-established from stock elsewhere.

The main threats are invasive species: black rats, feral goats and pigs, cats and dogs, and fire ants. Removing them is the groundwork for any return. Project Isabela cleared goats and pigs from key islands, and rats have been eradicated from sites including North Seymour and Pinzón in 2012. That cleared the way for the 2019 reintroduction on Santiago. It is unglamorous, decade-long work — and the reason land iguanas walk some of these islands at all today.

Where can I realistically see a land iguana — and what rules apply?

Several National Park visitor sites let you walk among land iguanas: Seymour Norte on North Seymour, Plazas Sur on South Plaza, Cerro Dragón on Santa Cruz, Punta Espinoza on Fernandina and Santa Fe-Fondeadero on Santa Fe. Which of these you reach comes down to your specific itinerary; several routes cover at least one iguana site.

Wherever you go, the same rules apply. A GNPS-certified naturalist guide is required in protected areas, you must stay at least 2 metres from wildlife, and there is no touching, feeding or flash photography — stay on the marked trails. No operator can promise a specific animal on a given day; wildlife moves on its own schedule.

Key takeaways

  • The land iguana is a large, yellowish, plant-eating endemic lizard — the sea-feeding marine iguana is a separate species.
  • It lives on Isabela, Fernandina, Santa Cruz, Baltra, South Plaza and North Seymour, with native, reintroduced and introduced populations mixed together.
  • North Seymour's iguanas were brought from Baltra in the 1930s; Santiago's were reintroduced in 2019; Baltra's were re-established after the roughly 1954 extinction.
  • The pink land iguana lives only on Wolf Volcano (about 350, Critically Endangered) and its range is closed to visitors.
  • Opuntia cactus is central to the diet; Santa Fe pairs a distinct iguana with giant Opuntia.
  • Walking encounters are realistic at Cerro Dragón, Plazas Sur, Seymour Norte, Punta Espinoza and Santa Fe-Fondeadero — under a certified guide, at least 2 metres away.

Frequently asked questions

What is the Galápagos land iguana?

A large, yellowish, endemic lizard that lives on land and eats mainly plants, especially Opuntia cactus. It is one of four iguana species in the archipelago and lives on several islands — some populations natural, some reintroduced.

What's the difference between a land iguana and a marine iguana?

The difference is where they feed. The marine iguana is the only lizard that feeds in the sea, grazing on underwater algae. The land iguana stays ashore and eats cactus and other plants. Both are endemic, but they fill completely different niches.

Which islands have Galápagos land iguanas?

They live on Isabela, Fernandina, Santa Cruz, Baltra, South Plaza and North Seymour. The populations are a mix: some native, Baltra and Santiago reintroduced, and North Seymour's brought from Baltra in the 1930s.

Can you see the pink land iguana in the Galápagos?

Not on a normal trip. The pink land iguana — a separate species of about 350 animals, Critically Endangered — lives only on Wolf Volcano, Isabela. That range is permit-restricted and closed to general visitors, so it is not a sighting you can plan for.

What do Galápagos land iguanas eat?

They are herbivores whose diet leans heavily on the Opuntia (prickly-pear) cactus — pads, flowers and fruit. On arid islands with little standing water, the moisture and nutrition in cactus make it central to survival. Santa Fe pairs a distinct land iguana with giant Opuntia.

Are North Seymour's land iguanas native?

No. They were introduced — brought over from Baltra in the 1930s — so they are not a native population. The island is also known for the largest magnificent-frigatebird colony and prime seabird breeding.

Where can I see a land iguana in the Galápagos?

Walking encounters are realistic at Cerro Dragón (Santa Cruz), Plazas Sur (South Plaza), Seymour Norte (North Seymour), Punta Espinoza (Fernandina) and Santa Fe-Fondeadero (Santa Fe). Which you reach depends on your itinerary. A certified guide is required and no sighting is guaranteed.

Why did land iguanas go extinct on Baltra?

Baltra's land iguanas disappeared around 1954, in line with the pressures that have hit ground-dwelling reptiles across the islands, including introduced species. The population was later re-established from stock elsewhere — one of several successful iguana reintroductions in the archipelago.

How close can you get to a land iguana?

National Park rules require you to stay at least 2 metres from all wildlife, with no touching, feeding or flash photography, and to keep to marked trails under a GNPS-certified naturalist guide. The iguanas are often unbothered, but the distance rule always applies.

Is the Galápagos land iguana endangered?

The separate pink land iguana is Critically Endangered, with only about 350 individuals on Wolf Volcano. Land iguanas overall have faced local extinctions and invasive-species pressure, but reintroductions on Baltra, Santiago (2019) and elsewhere have re-established populations.
